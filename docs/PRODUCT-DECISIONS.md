# Noto — Product Decisions

The important decisions in this project, and why they were made — not just what was built.

## Evidence as a first-class object

**Decision:** every extracted item carries its source quote, timestamp, and speaker as a permanent, visible field — not metadata you have to dig for.

**Why:** a PM (or anyone reviewing the plan) should be able to verify where an extracted item came from without re-reading the whole transcript. If the product asks for trust, it should make verification effortless, not optional.

## No meaningless confidence scores

**Decision:** Noto never shows a percentage confidence score (e.g., "82% confident"). Instead, uncertainty is surfaced as a specific, named state: `owner_unclear`, `deadline_unclear`, `conflicting_statements`, or an item marked for evidence review.

**Why:** "82% confident" doesn't tell a reviewer what to actually check. A named ambiguity type does — it points directly at the thing that needs a human decision, instead of asking them to guess what the number means.

## Human approval before authoritative output

**Decision:** nothing generated by Noto is treated as real until a person approves it. Approval is a required step, not a fallback for when the AI seems unsure.

**Why:** meeting interpretation has operational consequences — a wrong owner or a fabricated decision doesn't just look bad, it can cause real work to happen (or not happen) incorrectly. The cost of a bad automated call is higher than the cost of asking a human to confirm it.

## Copy-ready Jira/Notion instead of live integrations

**Decision:** exports are formatted, copy-ready text (Jira ticket markup, Notion-ready markdown) rather than live API calls to Jira or Notion.

**Why:** this MVP is testing whether the *extraction and review* workflow is actually useful before adding integration surface area, auth, and API maintenance for two more external services. Building the integrations before validating the core value would be solving a problem I hadn't confirmed existed yet.

## Preserve superseded decisions

**Decision:** when a decision is explicitly reversed later in the same meeting, Noto keeps both the original and the final decision, linked together, instead of silently replacing one with the other.

**Why:** deleting or overwriting the earlier decision hides *how* the team got to the current answer. That history is often exactly what someone needs later ("didn't we originally say we'd use polling? why did that change?").

## Conservative behavior under uncertainty

**Decision:** when the system can't confidently determine an owner, a deadline, or whether something was actually decided, it defaults to flagging it for human review rather than making its best guess.

**Why:** for an execution-tracking workflow, an explicit "needs clarification" is a safer failure mode than a fabricated certainty that someone downstream trusts and acts on.

## Preference ≠ decision

**Decision:** a preference, proposal, or discussion point is never automatically promoted to a finalized decision — even when it's the only candidate the AI extracted for that topic.

**Why:** this came directly from a real failure in user testing (see `docs/USER-RESEARCH.md`). Preferences and proposals are extremely common in real meetings, and a product whose entire value proposition is "don't invent what wasn't agreed" cannot afford to treat "I'd prefer X" as equivalent to "we're doing X." This is arguably the single most important product insight to come out of this whole project.

## Deterministic fallback instead of a hard AI dependency

**Decision:** if the Gemini call fails or is unavailable (no API key, network error, quota, malformed response), the app falls back to a fully deterministic, rule-based extraction engine rather than failing or showing an empty state.

**Why:** a portfolio/demo product that hard-fails without a live API key isn't a good demonstration of anything. It also forced a useful architectural constraint: both extraction paths (AI and deterministic) had to produce the exact same `ExecutionItem` shape, which kept the downstream review/export logic simple regardless of which engine produced an item.
